Worked Case Study: Translating SWE-bench % into Real Task Cost

A 5-percentage-point difference on SWE-bench Verified looks small on paper, but in automated CI pipelines it directly alters retry multiplier costs. Suppose your agent runs 1,000 bug-fix tasks per month, with each failed attempt triggering up to 2 automated retries plus a senior engineer triage pass:

When GPT-5.6 Luna scores 62.7% at $0.20/$1.20 compared to Claude Fable 5.1 at 68.4% ($10/$50), running a two-stage cascade—attempting the patch first with Luna and escalating only the ~37% failures to Fable 5.1 or Opus 5—captures nearly frontier-level resolution rates at less than 25% of the pure flagship budget.

How to read this matrix

The rows are limited to results published together with enough shared context to support a narrow comparison. A higher percentage means more tasks met that benchmark's scoring criteria in the cited run; it does not prove that the model will perform better in your repository, language, tool environment, or review process.

Comparable does not mean independent

The values are vendor-published. AI Agent Hub did not rerun the benchmark, verify every patch, or audit the evaluation harness.

Configuration matters

Agent scaffolding, reasoning budget, tool access, retries, model version, and inference settings can materially change a score.

Cost is incomplete here

The displayed unit prices do not include how many calls or tokens the benchmark run consumed. Do not infer cost per solved issue from this table.

Use benchmarks to form a shortlist

  1. Filter to models that meet your context and price constraints.
  2. Read the cited source and confirm the exact benchmark version and setup.
  3. Test the shortlist on representative tasks from your own workflow.
  4. Measure accepted outcomes, retries, latency, tool steps, and human review time.

Common interpretation mistakes

  • Comparing scores from different benchmark versions as if they used the same task set.
  • Treating a vendor result as an independent laboratory finding.
  • Ignoring contamination, harness changes, or missing confidence intervals.
  • Choosing a model from one score without testing safety and operational reliability.
